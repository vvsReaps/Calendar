LIQUIDITY / field guide
Liquidity Pools Premium / Discount Sweep / Run Resistance Structure Rules FAQ
ICT concept · order flow & resting orders

Every old high and every old low
is somebody's stop.

Price doesn't move at random. Underneath every candle is a market built from resting orders — buy stops above old highs, sell stops below old lows, breakout traders waiting to be triggered. Price is drawn toward those pools because that's where the volume is.

This guide walks through how ICT (Inner Circle Trader) traders read that order flow: where liquidity pools form, how a sweep differs from a run, why some levels resist longer than others, and how market structure ties it all into a single, readable narrative.

01 · Foundation

What liquidity actually means.

Liquidity means the willing buyers and sellers of an asset at the current market price — the degree to which it can be bought or sold without materially moving that price. According to Michael Huddleston (ICT), liquidity in trading refers specifically to the buy and sell orders sitting in the market at any given moment.

Retail traders overwhelmingly transact at market. Most will buy above an old high once it breaks — a breakout entry — and sell below an old low the same way. Anyone already in a position does the mirror version: a trader who bought earlier places a protective sell-stop below the old low, and a trader who sold earlier places a protective buy-stop above the old high. Layer enough of this retail behavior on top of itself and you get two very predictable shelves of resting orders sitting just outside the current range.

That's the entire premise ICT liquidity concepts are built on: price seeks liquidity. A market doesn't need a news catalyst to move toward an old high or old low — the orders resting there are often reason enough. Institutional-size participants can't fill large positions at one price without moving the market against themselves, so they rely on pools of opposing retail orders to provide the other side of the trade. That's what "smart money buys below market and sells above market" really describes in practice: it isn't magic, it's using the liquidity that's already sitting there.

Once you can see the market this way, price stops looking random. Every old high and every old low becomes a landmark — not because of any indicator, but because you already know, structurally, that orders are resting on the other side of it.

Retail behavior

Orders cluster at extremes.

Breakout buyers enter above old highs, chasing confirmation of strength. Breakout sellers enter below old lows the same way, chasing confirmation of weakness.

Anyone already positioned puts protective stops on the other side of those same levels — buy-stops above highs to cap a short's losses, sell-stops below lows to cap a long's losses. Both groups end up contributing to the same two pools.

Smart money behavior

It trades against the crowd.

Because those levels are obvious to everyone watching the same chart, they become predictable. Price is drawn toward the highs and lows not by coincidence, but because that's where the volume needed to fill large orders is already sitting, unfilled and waiting.

This is why ICT concepts frame smart money as buying below market value and selling above it — it is quite literally transacting opposite the crowd's stops and breakout entries.

02 · Buy-side vs sell-side

The pool is named for what's inside it.

The area above an old high and the area below an old low are each termed a liquidity pool — a concentration of resting orders waiting to be filled. Buy-side liquidity rests above old highs in the form of buy-stops and eager breakout buyers. Sell-side liquidity rests below old lows in the form of sell-stops and eager breakout sellers.

Liquidity pools — buy-side above the old high, sell-side below the old low.
Buy-side liquidity — rests above old highs
Sell-side liquidity — rests below old lows

In an uptrend, smart money's objective is usually to buy at a discount — that means dipping into sell-side liquidity below a recent low before continuing higher. In a downtrend, the objective flips: sell at a premium by trading up into buy-side liquidity above a recent high before continuing lower. Reading a pool correctly means asking two questions at the same time: which side is this level's liquidity resting on, and does the higher-timeframe trend actually want to reach for it right now?

Example — sell-side swept, price rallies into buy-side.

Bullish pool → buy the discount.

Bullish liquidity pools are areas where smart money looks to buy. Below the old lows, sell-side liquidity rests in the form of sell-stops from trapped longs and eager breakout sellers.

  1. Price trades below an old low, clearing sell-side liquidity — this is the discount zone.
  2. You're now buying against the sell orders of trapped retail traders, not against the trend.
  3. Price is drawn back up toward the old highs — buy-side liquidity — completing the round trip.
Typical invalidation ≈ 40–50 pips below entry — price shouldn't need to travel much further below the swept low if the read is correct. Target: the prior buy-side pool.
Example — buy-side swept, price sells off into sell-side.

Bearish pool → sell the premium.

Bearish liquidity pools are areas where smart money looks to sell. Above the old highs, buy-side liquidity rests in the form of buy-stops from trapped shorts and eager breakout buyers.

  1. Price trades above an old high, clearing buy-side liquidity — this is the premium zone.
  2. You're now selling against the buy orders of trapped retail traders, not against the trend.
  3. Price is drawn back down toward the old lows — sell-side liquidity — completing the round trip.

03 · Where inside the range

Buy cheap. Sell expensive.

Premium and discount describe where price sits inside a meaningful range, usually split around its midpoint. They give the liquidity pools above and below a range their trading context.

Take any swing high to swing low and split it in half. The lower half — closer to the low — is the discount. The upper half — closer to the high — is the premium.

A bullish bias wants to buy from discount, ideally after sell-side liquidity below the range has already been swept. A bearish bias wants to sell from premium, ideally after buy-side liquidity above the range has already been swept.

This is what keeps a liquidity read honest. A sweep at the lows only becomes an attractive long if it's also happening in a discount relative to the larger range — otherwise you're buying into resistance, not into an inefficiency the market still needs to close.

04 · Two outcomes at a level

Does price take the liquidity — or keep going?

A sweep and a run look identical in the first second. Both approach a known level with intent. What separates them is what happens the moment after the level is touched — and knowing which one you're watching changes everything about how you should react.

Liquidity Sweep

Price pokes through, then reverses.

An ICT liquidity sweep refers to a price movement designed to capture liquidity before reversing direction. Price aims for equal highs, targeting buy-side liquidity, and momentarily surpasses them — but fails to close above, and quickly reverses.

Alternatively, price can close above the highs and still reverse shortly after, driven by strong opposing momentum. Either way, the move exists to capture liquidity, not to continue — that's what makes it a sweep and not a breakout.

Liquidity Run

Price takes it, then continues.

An ICT liquidity run occurs when price moves in the direction of the prevailing trend, targeting a level, capturing the liquidity resting there, and continuing its movement without reversing.

In a bullish market, price aims for a previous high, captures the liquidity there, and keeps rising toward a new high. In a bearish market it does the mirror version at a previous low. Nothing about the level stops the move — it fuels it.

→ On the chart

The same two ideas, in real price.

To anticipate either one, you need a clear read of the higher-timeframe market structure first. If it's bullish and price is testing a higher-timeframe discount, expect a sweep of equal lows before reversing up. If it's bearish and price is testing a higher-timeframe premium, expect a sweep of equal highs before reversing down. Once liquidity has already been swept at the origin of the move — commonly called inducement — the continuation that follows is read as a run, not a fresh sweep.

Bearish structure — sweep at the highs, reversal down.

Price should test the higher-timeframe premium by sweeping equal highs or a bullish liquidity pool — then reverse downward. Once that sweep has happened, subsequent pushes to new lows are read as a run, for the same reason.

05 · How hard is the level defended

Not every level gives up its liquidity easily.

An old high with many swing highs defending it, or an old low with many swing lows defending it, is termed high resistance liquidity. A level with only a few thin swings between price and the target is termed low resistance liquidity. Knowing which kind of level you're targeting changes how much time and conviction the trade requires.

HRLR

High Resistance Liquidity Run

A high resistance liquidity run occurs when price struggles to sweep out liquidity and takes a long time to reach an old swing high or low, because the level is defended by multiple layered resistance points along the way.

If price is making higher highs and higher lows in a clear uptrend after taking sell-side liquidity, it will typically face difficulty dropping all the way back down through the long-term low, simply because there's too much stacked structure in the way. This resistance can hold — sometimes only a scheduled catalyst like NFP, FOMC, or CPI has enough force to finally drive price through it. HRLR setups are generally considered less favorable to trade, because reaching the targeted liquidity can take considerably longer, or may not happen cleanly at all.

LRLR

Low Resistance Liquidity Run

The short-term swing highs and lows forming between an old high and an old low are termed low resistance liquidity, because they're easy to sweep out — there's nothing substantial defending them.

Unlike a high resistance run, a low resistance liquidity run features aggressive acceleration, often leaving fair value gaps or liquidity voids behind as price moves quickly and efficiently through the level. In a bearish trend, price makes lower short-term lows, so it's easy for price to run below a recent short-term low and sweep out sell-stops. In a bullish trend, the same logic applies above recent short-term highs. LRLR setups are the easiest, quickest, most effortless conditions to trade — and generally carry the higher probability of success.

Order flow decides which is which. HRLR and LRLR aren't fixed properties of a level — they depend on the prevailing institutional order flow. In bullish order flow off a long-term low, every swing high should be treated as a low resistance liquidity run, while the long-term low itself is the high resistance liquidity.

In bearish order flow off a long-term high, the relationship flips: every swing low is low resistance, and the long-term high becomes the high resistance liquidity. In practice, traders lean on LRLR targets for quick, simple trade setups, and treat HRLR targets as longer-term objectives that may need a fundamental event to align with their bias before they're actually reached.

06 · Reading the swings

Three tiers of high, nested inside each other.

Market structure is the framework within which a market is trading at any given time — it provides insight into the market's behavior, condition, and current flow. Swing highs and swing lows are the raw material; short term, intermediate term, and long term highs are how ICT organizes them into something you can actually read.

Market structure has three broad types. Bullish structure means consistently rising prices, shown by higher long-term highs and higher long-term lows. Bearish structure means consistently falling prices, shown by lower long-term lows and lower long-term highs. Sideways structure means price is trading in a range, repeatedly making equal highs and equal lows without committing to a direction.

01
Short Term High — STH

Basically an ICT swing high: a three-candle formation where the high (wick) of the middle candle is higher than the high of both the candle on its left and the candle on its right. The high of that middle candle is what gets marked as the STH.

02
Intermediate Term High — ITH

"Intermediate" means sitting between two stages. An ITH is a short term high that itself has a lower short term high on both its left and right side — the middle of two short term highs, and higher than both of them.

03
Long Term High — LTH

An LTH is an intermediate term high in nature, but it's usually formed at a higher-timeframe PD array after a reaction of price. It's indicated by an intermediate high sitting in the middle of — and higher than — two other intermediate term highs.

In bullish market structure, price makes higher long-term highs and higher long-term lows. If price is supposed to keep going higher, it should not violate the most recent higher long-term low along the way. If it does break that low, the structure is no longer bullish — that single rule is often the cleanest, least subjective way to tell a healthy pullback from an actual trend change.

Bullish structure

Higher long-term highs and higher long-term lows. A break below the most recent long-term low ends the bullish read.

Bearish structure

Lower long-term lows and lower long-term highs, mirroring the bullish case in reverse.

Sideways structure

Price ranges between roughly equal highs and equal lows — no directional bias established.

07 · Non-negotiables

Liquidity is context — not a signal on its own.

None of the concepts above are meant to be traded in isolation. A liquidity pool tells you where orders are resting. A sweep or run tells you what happened when price got there. Resistance tells you how much conviction that level needed. Structure tells you the story those pieces sit inside. Put together, they build a case for a trade — on their own, each one is just a single clue.

01
Know the higher-timeframe bias first

A sweep only means what you think it means if it agrees with the prevailing bullish or bearish structure above it. Reading a sweep against the higher-timeframe trend is how a valid concept turns into a losing trade.

02
Identify buy-side and sell-side pools before price arrives

Mark the old highs and old lows in advance. You should already know where the pools are resting well before price gets there — reacting after the fact defeats the purpose of the concept.

03
Watch for sweep vs run — don't assume

A reversal after the level is swept is sweep behavior; continuation through it is a run. The two look identical for the first candle or two, so wait for confirmation rather than guessing which one you're in.

04
Weigh resistance before targeting

Low resistance liquidity is the easiest, highest-probability target and usually the better trade. High resistance liquidity may take longer, and may need a fundamental catalyst to actually be reached.

05
Confirmation is optional, risk management is not

No confirmation is strictly required to trade a liquidity pool — the model assumes price will seek and return from the highs or lows on its own. What is required is a defined stop, typically around 40–50 pips beyond the swept level, sized to what you can genuinely afford to lose.

06
Size risk to the level, not to conviction

Standard practice is to risk only around 1% of capital per trade, regardless of how confident the setup feels. Conviction doesn't change how far price should reasonably travel past a swept level — the level does.

08 · Common questions

Before you go looking for a trade.

01Do I need confirmation to trade a liquidity pool?

No — the ICT liquidity pool model doesn't require a confirmation candle or signal before entry. What it does require is a defined stop loss, typically 40–50 pips beyond the level, because price is expected to seek liquidity and then return from the highs or lows rather than travel far past them.

02How much should I risk per trade?

Only risk what you can genuinely afford to lose. A standard guideline is around 1% of capital per trade — a level that lets a normal losing streak happen without threatening the account, regardless of how a single setup looks.

03Is the liquidity pool model actually reliable?

It's considered one of the more reliable models within ICT teaching, but it isn't reliable in isolation. Results depend heavily on having a correct read of higher-timeframe direction and bias first — the pool tells you where liquidity is, not which way price is ultimately headed.

04What's the practical difference between HRLR and LRLR when picking a target?

LRLR targets are the path of least resistance — quick, simple, and generally higher probability, which is why many traders default to them for take-profit placement. HRLR targets are more defended, may take longer to reach, and sometimes need a scheduled news catalyst to actually get swept — treat them as longer-range objectives rather than the first target in a trade plan.
